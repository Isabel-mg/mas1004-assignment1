\section*{Problem 4. Cleaning}
\subsection*{My rule}
An image stays only if it is an ordinary photograph of the outsole of the right shoe, where the tread pattern is visible and its shape can be told apart. Each category must show: Converse, a sole with the diamond shape; Nike Air Force, a sole with clean lines and circular shapes; Vans, a sole with the honeycomb shape. I remove an image if it (1) shows the full shoe and the sole is out of frame, tiny or blurred; (2) shows something other than the outsole of a shoe (drawing, logo, text, collage, screenshot); (3) shows a sole that does not match the brand and the specific model of its category; (4) shows anything else that is not a shoe; (5) shows a sole that is destroyed, covered or mixed with other objects so that no pattern or shape can be seen; (6) is a near copy of an image I already keep. I keep angled soles as long as part of the pattern is visible and its shape can be told apart, worn or dirty soles unless no shape or pattern can be seen at all, and two soles in one picture if both are the correct brand and model. The full rule is in \texttt{outputs/rule.txt}. I wrote this rule before removing anything, and these are the rules I used for all the cleaning.

\subsection*{Limits of these tests}
These limits come from how the changes were made, and they apply to the conclusions below.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The grids, the inversion and the zoom change several things at once. A 12x12 grid adds many straight edges as well as covering part of the picture, so a result cannot say which of the two caused it. Inverting the colors changes colour, light and shadow together; the grayscale copies keep the light and shadow and remove the colour, which is the closest comparison I have.
  \item The model sees only the middle square of each photo after resizing. For the ``first third only'' and ``last third only'' copies, only 24\% to 63\% of the visible strip is inside that square (24\% for the two tall Converse photos, 48\% for three others and 63\% for the Nike photo 1791805866921). The grey part is also seen by the model.
  \item The shuffle cuts tall rectangles, not squares, and mixes background tiles with sole tiles, so which sole tiles end up in the middle square affects the answer.
  \item Six photos per change (two per category) is a small sample, and the Vans class has only five new photos in total.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{What I now think the model uses}
Based on my trials, I am around 90\% confident that color, lighting, and overall shoe context are not the main factors behind the model's predictions. Instead, the results suggest that it may rely on local texture patterns to distinguish Vans and Converse. For example, it could be detecting features such as edges and line intersections in the diamond-shaped Converse pattern or the hexagonal Vans pattern. This would explain why it can still recognize these shoes in zoomed-in or shuffled images, but sometimes confuses them with everyday objects that have similar textures, like a metal mesh fence. Nike appears to behave differently. Its predictions seem more dependent on the overall structure of the sole, particularly continuous curved lines and larger geometric patterns. This could explain why zooming in did not prevent correct classification, while shuffling the image tiles caused more mistakes by breaking the continuity between parts of the sole. The difference between trials 10 and 11 also suggests that preserving local patterns alone may not be enough for Nike classification.

Every conclusion rests on six photos per change (ten for the shuffle), so it is an indication, not a proof. The 10 original photos were all answered correctly by the model before any change (80.7\% to 100.0\%).
